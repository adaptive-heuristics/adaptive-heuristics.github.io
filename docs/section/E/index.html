E Robustness to Alternative Mappings between Heuristics and Firm Characteristics

Our measures of heuristic effectiveness depend on the firm characteristics we assign to each heuristic in Table 5 of the main paper. Because several firm characteristics can be related to the same heuristic, these assignments involve judgment. We therefore construct alternative mappings with LLMs, recalculate heuristic effectiveness under each mapping, and repeat our main tests.

We let LLMs stand in for the investors who use each heuristic. For one heuristic at a time, an LLM chooses the firm characteristics it would use to select stocks, either as a generic retail investor or with the background of an interviewed investor who uses that heuristic. It never sees our mapping or our results. We collect 10,112 such responses from two LLMs and two prompts and combine them into two kinds of alternative mappings, a consensus mapping for each LLM and prompt that keeps the most common choices and 7,596 distinct mappings that each combine one response per heuristic. We then ask how often our main conclusions survive when we replace our mapping with each alternative. The four steps below describe the procedure in detail.

  1. 1.

    Inputs and models. We use GPT-6.1 Sol and Claude Sonnet 5.5, both with high reasoning effort.7 Each call supplies the description of one heuristic from Table 3 of the main paper and a list of candidate characteristics. For each candidate, the model sees only the variable name, such as ni_be, and a definition based on JKP’s documentation of factors. JKP group their 153 factors into 13 clusters, and we draw the candidates for each heuristic from the clusters related to it. We exclude composite scores and heavily model-based measures because retail investors are unlikely to use them. For Buy-the-Dip and Dividends, whose clusters offer too few relevant candidates, we add a few closely related characteristics from the “Other Factors” list in the JKP documentation. The candidate sets are as follows.

    • For Fundamental Strength, we use the Profitability, Quality, Low Leverage, and Size clusters without the composite scores f_score, o_score, z_score, qmj, qmj_prof, qmj_growth, and qmj_safety (37 candidates).
    • For Growth/Innovation, we use the Investment, Profit Growth, and Size clusters (39 candidates).
    • For Blue-Chip Comfort, we use the Low Leverage, Low Risk, and Size clusters without the composite score z_score (33 candidates).
    • For Momentum, we use the Momentum cluster (8 candidates).
    • For Buy-the-Dip, we use the Short-Term Reversal cluster and add the past two- and three-month returns, ret_2_0 and ret_3_0 (8 candidates).
    • For Dividends, we use the four payout characteristics of the Value cluster, div12m_me, eqnpo_12m, eqnpo_me, and eqpo_me, and add the trailing one-, three-, and six-month dividend yields, div1m_me, div3m_me, and div6m_me (7 candidates).
    • For Valuation/Mispricing, we use the Value cluster without the model-based measures eq_dur and ival_me (16 candidates).

    The candidate sets span 124 distinct characteristics in the JKP global factor data and include every characteristic in our mapping.

  2. 2.
    Prompts and LLM calls. We use two prompts. Prompt A asks the model to act as a retail investor who applies the heuristic and not to invent personal characteristics. Prompt B adds the background of an investor we interviewed who adopts the heuristic, and tells the model to use this background as context for what the investor could understand and apply. Conditioning a language model on the background of real respondents brings its answers closer to those of the corresponding human subgroups (Argyle, Busby, Fulda, Gubler, Rytting, and Wingate, 2023) and diversifies them, so we can test our results on a wider range of maps. The background covers country, gender, age group, education, investment knowledge, investable assets, and risk attitude. For Prompt B, we make one call for each pair of an investor and a heuristic the investor adopts, so that the responses mirror the actual users of each heuristic. There are 2,528 such pairs, because many investors adopt more than one.8 We make the same number of Prompt A calls for each heuristic. Each LLM therefore answers 2,528 calls per prompt, for 10,112 responses in total. Every call starts a new independent session. The instructions tell the model to base its choice only on the supplied information, without outside information or prior knowledge.
  3. 3.
    Responses and maps. Each response selects one to three characteristics, each of which can be used to screen or rank stocks for the heuristic. Combining one response from each of the seven heuristics in every possible way, we get 144, 360, 2,340, and 5,616 distinct maps for GPT Prompt A, GPT Prompt B, Claude Prompt A, and Claude Prompt B, respectively, and 7,596 distinct maps across the four setups. We count each distinct map once, however often the LLMs propose it, because our goal is to examine how much our findings depend on which plausible mapping a researcher might adopt. To summarize the typical choices of each LLM and prompt, we also form a consensus map that keeps, for each heuristic, the characteristics the responses select most often. We provide the four consensus mappings in Table E1.
  4. 4.
    Repeating tests. For each distinct map, we recalculate heuristic effectiveness and rerun the main tests exactly as in the main paper.

In Figure E1, we compare the estimates under every possible map with those under our mapping. Every map yields positive belief-accuracy slopes and positive historical delivery, whereas the objective-specific out-of-sample estimates spread more widely and fall below zero for a few maps. In Figure E2, we show the corresponding p-values. Apart from the returns slope of belief accuracy, which is not significant under our mapping either, p-values exceed 0.10 only for the out-of-sample tests for returns and stability, and only under some maps.

In Table E2, we report the percentage of distinct maps in each setup that support each main-paper result, which shows how sensitive our results are to the choice of map. A map supports a result if its estimate matches the corresponding main-paper estimate in sign and in statistical significance at the 10% level. A map also supports a result if its estimate favors our hypothesis more strongly by being significantly positive where the main-paper estimate is positive but not significant.

For belief accuracy and historical delivery, every map supports the pooled result and the result for each objective. In 640 of the 7,596 maps, the returns slope of belief accuracy even becomes significant. Out-of-sample delivery depends more on the mapping. Every map supports the pooled and upside results, whereas, depending on the setup, 83% to 100% of the maps support the returns result and 58% to 89% support the stability result. In the remaining maps, the estimate is positive but not significant, except in 79 Claude-based maps, where it is negative but not significant. Thus, belief accuracy, historical delivery, and pooled out-of-sample delivery do not depend on our choice of characteristics, whereas out-of-sample delivery for returns and especially stability is more sensitive to it.
